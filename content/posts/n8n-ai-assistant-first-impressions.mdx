## Introduction

Years ago I modelled a university admissions process for a client in Australia. It took workshops and specialist tooling, and the deliverable was a whole suite of process models that our webMethods team then built. Recently, with that old work as inspiration, I gave n8n's new AI assistant, currently in preview, a brief for a simpler admissions flow and had it running in about two hours.

That gap is the subject of this post. It is a first-impressions piece on n8n, a tool I had never opened before, but what stayed with me was less the tool than what it implies for the analyst using it. Rapid prototyping, token costs recorded per step and a business case you can build inside the workflow itself point at a different job: not a business analyst handing over a specification, but what I have started calling an Agentic Analyst.

The short version is that I was impressed. The longer version, including where I think a human still has to sign, follows.

It is not a like-for-like rebuild, and it was never meant to be. But the contrast, a formal modelling exercise then and a described-and-running workflow now, is the whole story in two pictures.

I was not trying to ship a production system in an afternoon. I wanted to see how much of a working admissions flow I could stand up as a first test. It breaks into three stages:

- **Intake and understanding.** Gather the applicant's details, whether they arrive by form, email, another workflow or even social media, then read their transcript.
- **Screening and decision.** Match the application against the admission rules, then let a human make the call on anything that is not clear-cut.
- **Offer and enrolment.** Make the offer, wait for the applicant's response, then register and confirm the accepted student.

Throughout, the flow keeps applicant records in n8n's built-in Data Table and notifies staff over Slack and applicants over Gmail at each meaningful step. It is deliberately a mix of deterministic rules, AI-assisted judgement and human sign-off, rather than a straight-through automation.

## How the AI assistant handled it

I did not place a single node by hand to get going. I described the process in one line, a university admissions team from application through to enrolment, and rather than guessing, the assistant asked the shaping questions back: how applications arrive, where records should live, how people get notified, which stages to automate and how the decision should be made.

From those answers and a handful of follow-up prompts, about two hours later I had a working first cut: the form, the applicant emails (an acknowledgement carrying an application ID, then an offer that fired a trigger on acceptance), the Slack summaries for staff, a centralised rule set to screen against and the routing for clear passes, clear fails and everything in between. The AI steps that read and cross-checked the transcript slotted into the same flow.

For a first sitting with a tool I had never opened before, that is a striking amount of working process for the effort.

## What impressed me

My honest first reaction was, simply, wow.

Two things drove it. The first was the sheer reach for so little input. Everything in the walkthrough above, the form, the live email and Slack integrations, the rules and the routing, came from a conversation rather than a build manual.

The second was the build experience itself. It felt like a Figma for workflow design and build: fast, visual and forgiving, where you shape the thing in front of you rather than fight the tool. As someone who has spent years in and around process work, that immediacy is the part that stayed with me.

## Data and cost observability

One capability deserves its own mention: observability. n8n gives you step-by-step visibility into every node, the data going in and the data coming out, so you can see exactly what each step received and produced.

The part that caught my attention was cost. A business analyst has to put a benefit case behind a design, and the long-standing tool for that is activity-based costing: the end-to-end cost of a single process instance, historically dominated by systems, people and materials. In an agentic process, a meaningful slice of that cost moves to tokens.

n8n's experimentation mode fits this neatly. Each run records the tokens used, both the total for the whole workflow and the count for every individual node.

That changes when the costing happens. Instead of estimating token spend in a spreadsheet after the fact, an analyst can watch it accumulate node by node and tune the process for cost in real time, before the business case is even written.

## The role shift: from Business Analyst to Agentic Analyst

The takeaway I keep coming back to is what this does to the analyst's job.

Classic business analysis produces documents: requirements, process maps and specifications that someone else then builds. The Level 3 model earlier in this post is exactly that kind of artefact, precise and inert until a delivery team turns it into something real. Here, the same plain-language description of a process became the working process. The gap between describing it and running it collapsed.

That points at a role I would call the Agentic Analyst (AA). The skill is still analysis, understanding the outcome, the boundaries, the rules and the exceptions, but the deliverable is a live, agentic workflow rather than a specification handed downstream. The analyst who can describe a process clearly and orchestrate AI and human steps into something that actually runs is going to be valuable, and quickly.

## You still need to be technical, for now

I would not oversell the assistant. To get a result you would trust, you still need to be technically minded.

Knowing what a good process looks like, how data should flow, where a human belongs in the loop and what an integration or a credential actually needs still matters, and the assistant assumes a fair bit of it. The conversational front door is real, but the room behind it still rewards someone who understands the plumbing.

It will help you across that plumbing, to be fair. When I hit the Slack credentials, I just asked, and it walked me through OAuth2 against an access token and which one each Slack node needs.

## What about non-deterministic processing?

The obvious question, for anyone who works in a regulated process, is non-determinism. AI steps are not deterministic. The same transcript could be read slightly differently on two runs, and in admissions, where fairness and auditability matter, that is not a small thing.

What reassured me is that the design does not let the AI make the call. The consequential gates are deterministic or human: the admission rules screen against fixed thresholds, and the admissions decision is a person clicking approve or reject in Slack. The AI extracts and cross-checks the transcript, and the verify step exists precisely to flag where the extraction and the form disagree. The judgement sits with the rules and the people, not the model.

That does not make non-determinism free. AI steps still need guardrails: constrained prompts, structured outputs, a verification pass and a human in the loop for anything material. n8n makes those easy to place, but deciding where determinism is non-negotiable is a design decision, not a default. Knowing where a process can tolerate variation and where it absolutely cannot is exactly the judgement the Agentic Analyst has to bring.

## Goal-centric processes, and where a human belongs

That question gets bigger. There is a lot of energy right now behind goal-centric, dynamic processes, agents handed an objective and the freedom to reach it, in place of fixed deterministic flows. I agree there is real value there. But it sharpens the question of who owns a decision rather than removing it.

Set aside for a moment that European students fall under GDPR, which gives people the right not to be subject to decisions made solely by automated processing where those decisions significantly affect them, and a right to human intervention. Even without the regulation, there is a simpler test.

Picture yourself as the head of admissions. A mother is in front of you, in tears, asking why her child was declined. In the same breath she tells you the child's best friend, same school, same classes, lower grades, was accepted, and her own child, after twelve years of hard work, was not. Now try telling her that the AI made the call. You cannot. Someone has to be able to stand behind that decision, understand it and explain it to the person it lands on.

That is the balance. Automate the gathering, the checking, the routing and the drudgery, and let a process run goal-first where the stakes are low. But keep a human accountable for the decisions that change a life. These tools are ready to help with far more than most people expect. The judgement about where to draw that line is the human's job, and I would argue it always will be.

## Conclusion

Go back to the two pictures. The Level 3 map I drew years ago is still a good map, and the running workflow at the top of this post is the simpler flow it inspired, a couple of hours after describing it. What changed is not the analysis. It is that the analyst can now make it run.

That is why n8n suits the Agentic Analyst so well. It is a genuinely full lifecycle tool: design, build, run, observe and cost, all in one place. You can take an idea from a sentence to a running, costed workflow and walk into the room with a business case built on real token numbers rather than estimates.

None of that moves the human line. The rules still gate. A person still decides. And someone still has to be able to explain the outcome to the mother standing in front of them. Get that balance right and the plain-language front door is not a shortcut around good analysis. It is what good analysis produces now. I will be using it again.

<Terms>

Plain-English definitions for the terms and tooling in this post, listed alphabetically.

**Activity-based costing (ABC).** A costing method that builds up the end-to-end cost of a single process instance from the activities it consumes, traditionally systems, people and materials. In agentic processes a growing share of that cost is token usage.

**Agentic Analyst (AA).** My term for where the business analyst role is heading: the same analysis skill (outcome, boundaries, rules and exceptions), but the deliverable is a live, agentic workflow rather than a specification handed to someone else to build.

**AI assistant (n8n).** A preview feature in n8n that turns plain-language descriptions into workflow steps, generating and wiring nodes for you rather than requiring you to place each one by hand.

**Data Table (n8n).** n8n's built-in table storage for holding and tracking records inside a workflow, used here to keep applicant records as they move through the stages.

**DeepSeek.** A family of large language models, used here for the two AI steps that read and cross-check the transcript.

**GDPR.** The EU's General Data Protection Regulation. Among other things, Article 22 gives people the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing where those decisions significantly affect them, along with a right to human intervention.

**Level 3 process map (L3).** One level in a process hierarchy: a model showing the sub-processes and phases within a business process. Typically one of a full suite of models a process architect produces and hands to a delivery team.

**n8n.** A workflow-automation platform for connecting apps, data and logic into pipelines. Workflows are built from **nodes**, with a visual editor and a large library of integrations.

**Node.** A single step in an n8n workflow: a trigger, an action, a branch, or a call to an app, model or piece of logic.

**Token.** The unit of text a language model reads and generates. Model usage is metered and priced per token, so token counts are the running cost of an AI step.

</Terms>
